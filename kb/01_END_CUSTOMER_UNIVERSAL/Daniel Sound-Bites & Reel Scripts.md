## Antioxidants — SOD2 / GPX1

The oxygen you breathe is slowly poisoning you — but you have genes that protect you, assuming they work. 5% of inhaled oxygen is converted to aggressive free radicals. Left unchecked, they destroy cell walls, brain-cell ends, and proteins — one of the main causes of why we age. Genes like SOD2 and GPX1 neutralise them before harm. But not everyone has working versions. Many of us carry defects in these genes and lack this essential protection. The compensation: antioxidant nutrients can do the same job — vitamins C, E, polyphenols. So the right nutrition or supplementation gives your body the essential protection if your genes aren't up for the task.

## Aspirin and blood clots — ITGB3

Ever heard of people taking aspirin before long flights to prevent the "economy class syndrome" (a blood clot from sitting too long)? For some, this is a good idea. For others it is completely useless — and their genes are to blame. About 1 in 8 people of European descent have a defective ITGB3 gene, which completely abolishes the anti-blood-clot function of aspirin. Good news: if you are at risk, other medications work just as well or better. A perfect example of why prevention should be personalised.

## Athletic story 1 — Prescilla Lopez / LMNA

Prescilla was an exceptionally talented hurdles runner and Olympic medalist — but always accused of doping. Every doping test came back negative. Until a gene test gave the answer. She had a rare defect in the LMNA gene. In most cases this gene defect causes severe disease — in Prescilla it produced more muscle mass and less fat under the skin, leading to her distinctive physique. There is hardly any stronger genetic discrimination than competitive sports. Discipline and hard training are essential — but when everyone trains as hard as you, it is having the right genes that makes you a champion.

## Athletic story 2 — Eero Mäntyranta / EPOR

Blood doping (re-injecting your own red blood cells before a race) raises your red-blood-cell count by 10% — illegally. But Finnish cross-country skier Eero Mäntyranta had that advantage naturally. He won numerous Olympic medals and finished races with leads never seen before. A gene test discovered his hidden talent — a defect in the EPOR gene, which controls red-blood-cell production. Without any wrongdoing, Eero had 50% more red blood cells than his competitors — five times what blood doping can achieve.

## Athletic story 3 — Eddie Hall / MSTN (the Belgian Blue cow gene)

Ever heard of the body-builder cows? The Belgian Blue cows have three times the muscle mass of a normal cow, without steroids or training — they have a defect in the MSTN gene. In the body two opposing gene sets exist: ones that tell muscle to grow, and the MSTN gene that tells muscle to stop growing. When MSTN broke in the first Belgian Blue cow, the muscles lost the "stop" signal and grew to maximum. The world champion weightlifter Eddie Hall also has a defect in MSTN. Even without training he had more natural muscle mass. Discipline and training then made him the strongest weightlifter in the world. Genetics play a big role in elite sport.

## Dangerous cure — CYP2D6 / codeine

On the 12th day, the newborn baby died. Healthy at birth, but worsening day by day. Autopsy: morphine overdose. But neither baby nor mother had been treated with morphine. The only hint: the mother was taking codeine. Codeine itself is inactive in the body — it has no effect whatsoever. The CYP2D6 gene converts it to its active painkilling form, morphine, slowly over hours. The mother had a genetic peculiarity shared with 2% of the population — her CYP2D6 was hyper-active. All the codeine was converted to morphine all at once. The unnaturally high morphine spike ended up in the breast milk and slowly poisoned the baby. This is why knowing about your genes before taking any medication might save your life.

## Epigenetics 2 — identical twins separated at birth

What is a stronger influence: genetics or epigenetics? Identical twins separated at birth give us the perfect natural experiment — identical genes, different environment. Anything that comes out the same in both individuals must be strongly genetic; anything different must be environment/epigenetics. The findings are surprising: similar IQ, similar academic and language performance, the same personality traits (extraversion, neuroticism, openness, agreeableness), closely matching political and religious views, the same hobbies, similar jobs, choice of pets, favourite books, the same gait and posture, often the same diseases. Some pairs even married women with the same first name; the timing of marriage and divorce was similar; one pair each independently named their dog "Toy". This shows how deeply our genes influence every aspect of our lives. Epigenetics has a real effect — only in the differences that remain.

## Fat and muscle-mass loss during a diet

When you lose fat during a diet, you can also lose muscle. How much you lose is in your genes. With optimal genes, your body reduces fat while maintaining muscle — exactly what you want. With defective genes, your body loses fat AND muscle at an extended rate. With good genes you can do endurance exercise — burn the most calories in the shortest time and muscles are maintained anyway. With defective genes, protect your muscles via muscle-building exercise — push them to their limit so they don't break down. Your muscles won't significantly grow during a strict diet, but the loss can be stopped.

## Genes and burnout — COMT

Feeling stressed and easily overwhelmed? You might have a gene defect — the COMT gene. When you experience something exciting, your brain releases dopamine. Cell to cell, the whole brain gets bathed in dopamine and alerted. After the excitement, dopamine needs to be removed — that's COMT's job. If COMT works well: brain returns to normal. Add stress later: brain goes to 80%, COMT clears, all good. People with a defective COMT clear dopamine poorly — the brain stays bathed in excitement. Now add stress: brain was already at 80%, hits 100% and is overwhelmed. Studies show these people perform worse under stress and are more prone to burnout. Good news: certain nutrients, the right nutrition and lifestyle help even defective COMT do its job better. Your genes don't need to be your destiny.

## Genetic vs age-related cancer

What we usually call "genetic" cancer is when one gene is defective and causes the disease. Angelina Jolie is the classic example — a defective BRCA1 gene raised her cancer risk to 80%. But these single-gene cases are rare — only 1 in 650 women carry such a defect. "Genetic" breast cancer makes up only ~5% of all cases. The other 95% is age-related — and genes are also involved here, but differently. It's not single high-impact defects, it's many many genes each adding a small increase in risk. Individually a few percent each, but added together the risks become relevant. Lifestyle has a big impact — the right nutrition and lifestyle counteracts many of these small effects.

## Gluten — DQA1 / DQB1

Digestive issues, unwanted weight loss, cramps — symptoms of gluten intolerance. On average it takes 11 years before sufferers get the right diagnosis. You must have specific gene defects for celiac disease to even happen. The mechanism: APC cells gobble up invading bacteria, cut them into pieces, and two "docking arms" hold the pieces and present them: "look what our attackers look like". The immune system then learns the attacker and goes hunting. The two docking arms are built by DQA1 and DQB1 genes. Gluten normally doesn't fit these arms. Some people have special versions that can hold gluten like a bacterial fragment. The immune system is fooled — gluten is now "the invader" — and the cells attack the intestine. It is your genes that make you gluten intolerant. With normal genes, you are immune to this food intolerance.

## How to fix your genes — gene therapy

Here's how to fix a broken gene. We use a virus — nothing more than a self-replication machine. When a virus meets a cell it injects its own DNA. The cell follows the virus instructions blindly: make millions of new viruses. The cell produces viruses until it dies and releases them to infect more cells. Scientists figured out how to use this to our advantage. Start with someone who has a defective gene. Take a virus, remove its genes, replace them with a functioning human gene. Infect the cell — the virus injects the replacement gene and cures the cell. So far we can cure a handful of genes this way, but technology is moving fast — soon we'll be able to fix thousands.

## Lactose — LCT (the Scandinavian milestone)

Did you know being lactose-intolerant is actually normal? It's the adult milk-drinkers who are the genetic outliers. 20,000 years ago, babies got breast milk. Breast milk had lactose — too large to absorb in the intestine. The LCT gene split lactose into smaller absorbable sugars. As the baby grew up there was no more milk available, so the LCT gene developed an off-switch — turn off after childhood, save energy. The adult could no longer digest lactose, but there was no milk to drink so that was fine. Then we developed farming and kept animals for food. Adults had milk — but couldn't drink it. Lactose became food for gut bacteria — terrible digestive issues. This is what we today call lactose intolerance. About 6,000 years ago in Scandinavia, a gene defect happened — but a beneficial one. It didn't destroy LCT, it destroyed the off-switch. Suddenly this person could drink milk all his life. Famine killed many; the descendants with the new variant had an extra food source. Today 80% of Europeans can drink milk in adulthood. If you can drink milk and you're of European descent, we have a common Scandinavian ancestor.

## Leptin story — the Lisa and Thomas case

Imagine always being hungry — insatiable. By age ten, Lisa and Thomas both weighed more than three times what normal kids weigh. Their parents and older sister were fine. A genetic test gave the answer. Both kids had two defective copies of the LEP gene — the one that makes the hormone leptin. Leptin's job: suppress hunger. The mechanism is ingenious. LEP is active in fat cells, so the more fat cells you have, the more leptin you produce. More body fat → more leptin → hunger suppressed → fat reduces → leptin drops → you get hungry again → fat builds → stable base weight. With defective LEP, the system crashes. No leptin, no off-switch on hunger. The brain thinks the body is starving no matter how much fat is stored. Constant hunger, relentless weight gain. This defect is rare, but there are plenty of other gene defects that influence body weight. Being overweight is not just lack of discipline — it is rooted deep within us.

## Lipid Legacy — LDLR / familial hypercholesterolemia

High cholesterol that diet and exercise don't fix? You might have a defective LDLR gene. Normal LDLR builds a "fishing net" on the surface of liver cells, reaching into the bloodstream, capturing LDL cholesterol particles and pulling them into the cell. That's how the liver lowers unhealthy cholesterol. But 1 in 250 people have defective LDLR — the nets are malformed and don't work. The result: familial hypercholesterolemia. So high cholesterol is not always a lifestyle issue — it can be your genes.

## Man with two faces — UV-protection genes

The right side of this truck driver's face looks like a normal 60-year-old. The left side looks decades older. Reason: three decades of truck driving with only one side of his face in the scorching sun. Sun damage doesn't affect us all the same. We have inbuilt "sunscreen" in the form of certain genes. If they work, they protect from UV. If they're defective, skin is more sensitive to the sun. The compensation: actual sunscreen. If your genes don't protect you, you now know what to do.

## Smoking — CYP1A1 (and the oldest smokers ever)

Smoking is unhealthy. But the oldest woman and the oldest man to ever live were both smokers. Statistics say a smoking habit costs you about 10 years of life on average — but smoking doesn't harm us all the same. Inhaled smoke contains PAH toxins. The CYP1A1 gene recognises them and converts them to a first breakdown product. That intermediate is the actually-cancer-causing one — not the original toxin. Normally the body breaks the intermediate further and removes it. Some people have a hyper-active CYP1A1 variant — converts all the toxin at once. The body can't keep up with breaking down the intermediate. The result: a spike in cancer-causing substances. Studies show smokers with this defect have up to 30× higher lung cancer risk. So if you smoke (you shouldn't) — don't compare yourself to the old guy who's smoked 40 years and is fine. You might not be so lucky with your genes.

## The Asian Glow — ALDH2, beer culture vs tea culture

European love of beer and Asian love of tea has a genetic factor. In the 1800s London had a cholera epidemic — spread through contaminated water. 600+ died. But one building in the epicentre was magically protected — a brewery. If you take cholera-contaminated water and brew beer with it, it becomes safe. So while people around them died, the brewery workers were safe and drunk. People back then drank 6–8 litres of beer per day — but you needed good genes to handle that. Those who could, flourished. Those who couldn't drank water and got sick. This selected Europeans for stronger alcohol-handling genes. Asia also had cholera, but alcohol wasn't an option — up to 80% of some Asian regions have ALDH2 "super genes" that convert alcohol to its breakdown product aldehyde much too fast. The result: red flushing, then unconsciousness, before even feeling drunk. So Asians boiled water instead — for tea. Europeans built beer culture, Asians built tea culture — all because of one little gene.

## The Berlin Patient — CCR5 / HIV cured

In 2008 HIV was cured. Unfortunately only in one person — the "Berlin Patient". Timothy Ray Brown had HIV. Then he got leukaemia — his bone marrow was killing him. A bone marrow transplant was his only option. His doctor did something radical. He knew the virus also lived in the bone marrow — a normal donor would just let the virus reinfect. So he found a donor with a common genetic defect — a broken CCR5 gene. CCR5 is what HIV uses to enter cells. Without it, the virus is locked out. The ~1% of Europeans with only defective CCR5 genes are immune to HIV. After the transplant the HIV vanished. No meds. No relapse. Just gone. The leukaemia was also cured. Timothy was healthy again — the first human cured of HIV through genetics. Genetic science used in a novel way.

## The Celtic Curse — HFE / hemochromatosis

Jim O'Malley, a Pittsburgh police officer, was afflicted by the Celtic curse — hemochromatosis. The gene defect responsible developed in and was spread by the Celts. 10,000 years ago we were hunter-gatherers eating iron-rich animal food. The HFE gene set absorption at 10% of food iron. Then 7,500 years ago we began farming and ate mostly plant-based low-iron food. 10% was no longer enough — people became deficient, unhealthy, less fertile. 6,000 years ago in Ireland a beneficial gene defect happened. If you had one normal and one defective HFE, you absorbed 20% — twice as much iron. The Celtic carriers had the crisis averted. Since the variant was beneficial for fertility and during blood loss (battles), it spread through Europe. Today the variant is so common that some children inherit it from both parents — and absorb too much iron. It accumulates and damages joints and vital organs. Easily preventable: donate blood 4–6 times a year. Statistically you know 4 people with these defects who could prevent the disease if they knew. Genetic information is power.

## Vitamin D3 — VDR

You checked your Vitamin D3 — all in the normal range, so all good, right? The answer depends on your genes. Vitamin D3 is essential — it activates many healthy processes. For the body to recognise D3 and trigger the processes, it needs the VDR gene. VDR produces receptors — clasping arms with the right shape to hold D3. Once it attaches, the receptor sends a "do something healthy" signal. Too little D3 in blood → no signal → deficiency. Normal D3 in blood usually means you're ok. But about half of all people have defective VDR. The receptors are malformed — they still hold D3 but the signal they send is weaker. The cell doesn't recognise that there is enough D3. Simple fix: just raise D3. More of the malformed receptors get activated and together the signal is strong enough. These people need higher D3 blood levels for the same effect. A relevant genetic effect that is easy to fix — as long as you know.

## What is cancer

All our cells have a natural tendency to grow and replicate via "accelerator" genes. Great for bacteria; not what you want in a multicellular organism. So besides the accelerators, we have "suppressor" genes that tell cells to stop dividing. The balance keeps cell division in check. If you have a defect in one of these, things go wrong. Example: a UV ray hits skin and destroys the suppressor gene in one cell. The accelerators take over. The cell divides — now two. The two also lack the suppressor — divide again, four, eight, sixteen. A tumour grows. You now have skin cancer. The good news: we don't have just one accelerator and one suppressor — we have many. If one is knocked out, cancer is still prevented. The bad news: some people inherit defects in some of these defensive genes — their defences are lowered, so chance damage can quickly trigger cancer. This is why some families are more prone to cancer — fewer defensive genes protecting their cells.
