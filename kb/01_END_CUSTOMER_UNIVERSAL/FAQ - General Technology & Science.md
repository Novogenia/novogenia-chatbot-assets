### What is an SNP, and what is a gene mutation?

An SNP (Single Nucleotide Polymorphism) is a commonly occurring variation at a single position in the genetic code. SNPs are the primary differences between individuals and have the greatest influence on individual health. A gene mutation, by contrast, is a very rare, spontaneous error in the genetic code that typically occurs only in a single person or family.

### Why is a genetic test more useful than just a blood test?

A blood test provides a snapshot (current vitamin levels). A genetic test reveals innate metabolic properties — for example, whether the body can effectively utilize a nutrient, even when blood values appear normal. The VDR gene controls how well Vitamin D is absorbed into cells; if it carries a variation, deficiencies can exist even with normal blood levels. Both tests complement each other well.

### Can genes be changed by lifestyle?

The genes themselves do not change. Epigenetics — the regulation of genes (activation/silencing) — can be influenced by lifestyle, diet, and environment. These epigenetic changes can persist for many years, but they are not the same as a change to the genetic code itself.

## Additional FAQs


### What are gene mutations, and how can they affect an individual's health?

A gene mutation is a very rare, spontaneous error in the genetic programming of a person, which usually occurs only in one person or family. In most cases, doctors can only speculate about the impact of a mutation. This can either be positive or can trigger a disease.

### What are SNPs, and how do they contribute to genetic diversity and potential health impacts in humans?

These are individual changes in the genetic 'letter' code, which vary from person to person. These changes are common and occur in a certain percentage of the population. Typically, a SNP occurs every 50 genetic 'letters'. This means that every 50 bases there is a location where one letter is different than in a certain percentage of the population. If you were to overlay the genetic code of 1,000 people and look at the BRCA1 gene, you would see that this sequence is identical in all of them. The only difference would be the SNP locations. Statistically, 1 in 1,000 people will have such a rare mutation. SNPs have the greatest impact on a person's health and also make up the genetic difference from one person to another. Each of us is unique because of our unique mixture of SNPs. There is also a lot of research material on SNPs. For example, there are 294 studies with over 300,000 participants that show that differences in a SNP in the MTHF gene cause deficits in the conversion of folic acid. Most SNPs, however, have no impact on the human body; they are just there. This is important for later explanations.

### What is the significance of exome sequencing in genetic analysis, and what are the costs and benefits associated with this method?

This means that all 25,000 genes are analyzed, with each gene consisting of about 30,000 genetic letters (so-called bases). This is essentially the hard drive of the genetic code. However, the exome accounts for only 2% of the entire genetic code (which mainly consists of DNA junk). The genome would describe the entire genetic code. However, since genes are important to us, it is sufficient to take a closer look at the exome, as it contains the important data. In an exome genetic sequence, one could find information about 6,000 genetic diseases. Exomes are typically used to determine more severe medical effects, as these are often dependent on several genes. The reality is that only very rare diseases can be detected solely through exome sequencing, as most of these diseases are traceable back to SNPs.

### How does SNP genotyping work and what are its major advantages and applications in genetic research?

Genotyping refers to the study of variations in the genomes of different organisms. These variations often occur in the form of "single nucleotide polymorphisms" (SNPs), which are responsible for certain traits and also define or influence the general health of an organism. A single nucleotide polymorphism is, as the name suggests, a variation of exactly one base in the DNA. Normally, such SNP variants are inherited and thus passed on from generation to generation. Other genetic changes affect larger structures of the DNA, such as "copy number variations" (CNV). The analysis of SNP genotyping explores variations of a base in the genome from a group of individuals, where a PCR is typically used to determine different SNP profiles. Subsequently, a statistical analysis is conducted to associate the SNPs with specific traits or potentially calculate certain disease risks. SNP type assays use allele-specific primers for the detection of SNPs. Major advantages of the assays include the very short setup time for experiments, flexibility in the choice of assays, and reliability in the use of Dynamic Array IFCs. These assays are based on three primers and two universal probes to distinguish the two alleles of a gene. In SNP genotyping, labeled, allele-specific primers and reverse primers are used. The use of universal probe sets in each individual reaction ensures uniform fluorescence detection and thus a reliable determination of the SNP genotype.

### How do TaqMan assays and SNP type assays work, and what makes them suitable for detecting genetic variations?

TaqMan Assays: This assay is performed using TaqMan probes. TaqMan probes consist of oligonucleotides and have a fluorescent dye at both the end and the beginning. During the PCR, the TaqMan probes can bind to a defined region of the DNA. The associated DNA polymerase has a specific activity that is crucial for the probes. TaqMan probes have a special modification that prevents the extension of the base sequence by the DNA polymerase. Additionally, the probes have a binding element that targets the minor groove of the DNA helix and ensures better hybridization and more stable binding of the probes, allowing for the use of smaller probes. Two different probes are needed for this method. One probe is labeled with the fluorescent dye VIC and is responsible for detecting one of the two alleles. The other probe is labeled with the fluorescent dye FAM and is intended to detect the second allele. In homozygous samples, only one of the two dyes is measured, while in heterozygous samples, the two fluorescent dyes are present in approximately equal amounts. SNP type Assays: The mechanism underlying this assay is called allele-specific extension. For this method, in contrast to the TaqMan assay, 3 primers (two allele-specific primers (ASP1 and ASP2) and one locus-specific primer (LSP)) and two special reagents are used. The first reagent includes two universal probes. The probes consist of a specifically binding primer and are either bound with the fluorescent dye FAM or HEX. Furthermore, the second reagent contains a special molecule that forms a complex with the dyes of the probes. In the first two cycles of the PCR, the alleles are amplified. The new sequences now include the so-called "docking sequences" for the probes labeled with dyes. In the subsequent rounds of the PCR, the dye-labeled probe is integrated into the amplicon, generating a fluorescence signal.

### Why is genetic analysis important in assessing Vitamin D levels and its absorption influenced by genetics?

The question arises: “Why analyze genes when regular doctor visits already cover possible sources of various health risks?” In fact, a blood test is not sufficient for the analysis of necessary vitamins. In the following lines, I would like to explain using Vitamin D why this is the case... Vitamin D is a group of fat-soluble vitamins that belong to the secosteroids and plays a crucial role in regulating the calcium level in our blood and the associated bone formation. A Vitamin D deficiency can lead to rickets and osteoporosis in the medium term. A persistent Vitamin D deficiency is also considered a cause of various types of cancer (e.g., breast and colon cancer). The good thing is that the body can produce Vitamin D itself. However, sufficient Vitamin D supply requires adequate sun or UV-B exposure or a supportive diet. In food, Vitamin D is found in fatty fish (such as salmon, tuna, mackerel) or in milk, for example. If too little Vitamin D is produced, additional supplementation is possible or necessary. Adequate Vitamin D supply is, therefore, important for our long-term health. But how can your individual needs be determined? The usual way is through a blood test at the doctor’s - but be careful! When measuring the Vitamin D level in a blood test, it can lie within the recommended normal range, and yet a deficiency may still be present. The reason lies in the fact that so-called receptors are necessary for the absorption of Vitamin D. These receptors have two arms with which they capture Vitamin D and transport it into the cell. The formation of these receptors is determined by the VDR gene. If the VDR gene is “defective,” only one arm is formed instead of two, making it more difficult for the vitamin to be transported into the cells. However, studies have shown that in such cases, a significant increase over the generally recommended Vitamin D level can compensate for this deficiency (see Santos et al., 2012 and Zaki et al., 2017).
