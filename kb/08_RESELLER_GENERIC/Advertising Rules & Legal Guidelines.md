## Legal Background

### Important upfront

All advertising actions and competition-relevant statements for {{COMPANY_NAME}} products are solely the reseller's entrepreneurial risk. {{COMPANY_NAME}} cannot take responsibility for reseller advertising statements. These guidelines reflect 15 years of operational experience.

### The German Abmahnverein landscape

The health sector — particularly in Germany — is subject to strict advertising rules. Active warning associations ("Abmahnvereine") search for unclear or misleading statements and issue formal warnings. Most documented cases have emerged in Germany; other countries may be less stringent, but these guidelines represent careful best practice for any market.

### How a cease-and-desist warning works

A formal warning letter arrives claiming a statement is misleading, demanding a signed declaration (~€200 fee) and threatening a ~€5,000 penalty if the statement is used again. Options: sign the declaration (cannot use those statements again), or contest in court. If a warning is received: immediately notify {{COMPANY_NAME}} — support is available.

### Risk areas vs lower-risk areas

Higher risk: TV advertising, magazine inserts, social media ads (most warnings emerge here). Historically lower risk: websites and direct sales materials.

## Advertising with Blood Tests

### Permissible blood-test claims

Blood tests are long-established and widely accepted. Restrictions are minimal. Acceptable language: "We test blood / We diagnose deficiencies / Comprehensive report / Medical findings / We provide micronutrient or dietary recommendations / We personalise supplements based on results." Customer testimonials about symptom relief are also acceptable when supported by citing official sources.

## Advertising with DNA Tests

### What is unchallenged (safe)

Describing genetic traits and the analysis itself: "analysis and comparison of results with current science" and "determination of genetic traits" are accepted phrasings. Listing what genes are analysed is safe.

### What is challenged

Action programs derived from DNA — specific diet plans, exercise plans, micronutrient recommendations based on genes — are often challenged because studies confirm the genetic traits, but not always that the resulting recommendation achieves the stated outcome.

### Safe option — genetic traits only

Examples: "We analyse your genes to determine whether you gain weight from fats or carbohydrates." "You find out whether you respond better to exercise or calorie reduction based on your genes." "You learn whether your body can activate folic acid." "You discover what athletic talents your body has." These are descriptions of what the analysis reveals — not promises of outcomes.

### Bolder option — recommended by {{COMPANY_NAME}}

Advertise the genetic test and clearly label action recommendations as "logical conclusions" — not scientifically proven outcomes. Always include the disclaimer. Example: "We analyse your genes and, based on current science, determine whether you gain weight from fats or carbohydrates. Based on this, we create logical conclusions and nutritional plans to utilise these insights.*"

### Recommended disclaimer text (verbatim)

"That genes affect body weight, the effectiveness of certain weight loss strategies, and the ability to deal with certain nutrients is supported by multiple scientific studies for each gene. Therefore, the genetic traits determined by this analysis are considered scientifically confirmed. The adaptations of micronutrient dosing or dietary recommendations derived from these scientific findings have not yet been confirmed by randomised, placebo-controlled studies for all genetic effects. For this reason, the genetic adaptation of micronutrient dosing and food evaluation should be considered logical conclusions and not scientifically proven."

### Recommended structure for DNA-test advertising

1. Description: "We test DNA / match results with current science / comprehensive report of X pages." 2. Result interpretation (safe): description of genetic traits and findings. 3. Action recommendations (with disclaimer): "Based on this, we draw logical conclusions and create action plans*". 4. Customer testimonials (safe): "I wanted to know if folic acid might not work for me" / "I gained weight easily; the test showed me why". 5. Customer testimonials (avoid): "We help you lose weight" / "We personalise supplements according to your genes" without disclaimer.

## Advertising with Personalised Supplements

### EU Health Claims Regulation

The EU Health Claims Regulation defines precisely which effects can be claimed for dietary supplements. Only EFSA-approved Health Claims may be stated in advertising. Avoid claims outside the EFSA-approved list — these draw warnings.

### Safe supplement advertising structure

1. Product description: "X vitamins in handy daily pouches / Optimised absorption through quality raw materials and pellet format." 2. Personalisation (safe): "Personalised based on blood test results / Personalised based on lifestyle questionnaire." 3. Personalisation with disclaimer: "Logically / experimentally personalised based on genetic analysis*". 4. Promises of effect: EU Health Claims only. 5. Social proof: customer satisfaction statistics, reorder rates, recommendation rates.

### Handling customer reviews

If you operate your own review system: state clearly that reviews are customer opinions; delete reviews containing unauthorised efficacy claims; add a corrective company response if a review contains significantly incorrect information. If you use an external review system you cannot moderate: state clearly that reviews are customer opinions; do not filter or highlight selectively; attach corrective responses where possible.

## Practical Checklist Before Publishing

### Pre-flight checks

1. Does every health claim correspond to an EFSA-approved Health Claim or a clearly disclaimed "logical conclusion"? 2. Are competitor names absent from the material? 3. Are testimonials presented as customer opinions, not company claims? 4. Is the recommended disclaimer present in any DNA-action material? 5. Are images and graphics free of medical-effect claims not in the text? 6. Has a colleague reviewed the material with a sceptical eye?

### If unsure

Send the draft to {{COMPANY_NAME}} for review before publishing. Doing this once is much cheaper than receiving a warning later.
